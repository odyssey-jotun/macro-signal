What the research says about investors and the news

Before I built anything, I read what economists have found about how everyday investors handle financial information. Here is what the studies show.

More information, harder decisions

When an investing decision gets more complicated, people tend to put it off or take whatever is picked for them.

  • 75% → 60%

    More funds, fewer savers

    Across 647 retirement plans and almost 800,000 employees, 75% signed up when their plan offered two funds. When it offered 59, about 60% did.

  • 1.5–2%

    Every 10 funds

    Each 10 funds a plan added went with a 1.5 to 2 percent drop in sign-ups.

  • 34%

    “Do not know”

    Asked a basic question about spreading out investment risk, 34% of Americans answered “do not know.”

  • 20% vs 2%

    Overwhelmed people take the default

    In an experiment, people who knew less about investing took the default choice 20% of the time. People who knew more took it 2% of the time. The authors wrote that people with below-average knowledge “are simply overwhelmed by the investment decision.”

Sources: Sethi-Iyengar, Huberman and Jiang, 2004; Lusardi and Mitchell, 2014; Agnew and Szykman, 2005.

Trading more, earning less

Economists followed 66,465 households at a large discount broker from 1991 to 1996. The households that traded the most earned 11.4% a year. The market as a whole returned 17.9%.

A later study used every trade in Taiwan from 1995 to 1999. Individual investors lost 3.8 percentage points a year to trading, while institutions, with their analysts and software, gained 1.5.

Source: Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean, Review of Financial Studies, 2009.

Buying whatever grabs attention

Individual investors tend to buy the stocks in the news, and those stocks often do worse afterward.

  • 2 to 1

    Buying the buzz

    On days when a stock traded in unusually high volume, individual investors made nearly twice as many purchases of it as sales.

  • −4.7%

    The crowd's favorites

    The stocks Robinhood users bought in the biggest numbers each day trailed the market by an average of 4.7% over the next 20 trading days.

Sources: Barber and Odean, Review of Financial Studies, 2008; Barber, Huang, Odean and Schwarz, Journal of Finance, 2022.

What these studies can't tell us

These studies describe how real investors did, on average, in the past. They don't show that an AI can do better, and they don't say anything about any one investor.

That's the question my project is testing. Every trade the AI makes happens on a paper account with no real money, and I'll post the results each week, including the weeks it gets wrong. Paper results are hypothetical and can't predict future returns.
